Tarana vs 5G fixed wireless

In the Lowcountry the trees decide, not the towers. Every sub-7 GHz radio sees near free-space loss to 25 km over this ground (highest point in the county: 39 m). What separates the technologies is how many decibels each can spend on pine canopy, swamp forest and house walls at the customer's end, and whether the uplink survives the trip back.

Every number is tagged M measured, C claim-anchored or D derived here, with the source in the last tab. Coverage figures below are computed live from a Longley-Rice terrain run on three real FCC-registered towers plus a land-cover clutter model; change any assumption on the map tab and they move.

What the model says

Tarana's edge is real and it is mostly antenna physics. Sixteen radio chains at the base node and eight at the remote node buy roughly 9 dB of uplink and 3–4 dB of downlink over a WISP-grade 5G NR sector with a 17 dBi outdoor CPE, and the two to four 40 MHz carriers buy capacity on top. In this clutter that translates into about — more county homes at 100/20 from the same towers.

The larger lever is where the CPE sits. Moving an MNO C-band gateway from a window sill to a 17 dBi roof panel at 6 m roughly doubles its 100/20 footprint; raising Tarana's customer radio (the Remote Node, RN) from a 6 m eave mount to a 10 m pole adds another —. No operator gets around the pine canopy; they only choose how much of the link budget to spend on it.

What it means commercially

MNO macro FWA is a capacity product sold address by address into fallow mobile capacity. It wins on price and zero truck rolls, and it is already shrinking in cities where sectors fill up. Dedicated CBRS 5G NR reaches about two-thirds of Tarana's footprint for roughly 40% of the sector hardware cost, but with half the spectrum per sector and a 3GPP vendor base that just lost Cambium's cnRanger and never gained a strong CBRS small-cell champion.

Tarana is the performance leader and a single-vendor bet: a 4-BN G2 site is about $155k of radios, the RN costs 3–5× a 3GPP CPE, and the company is private, flat on its last three rounds, and dependent on CBRS and 6 GHz rules it does not control. mmWave FWA covers nothing here beyond the first few hundred metres of open ground.

How each system actually works

Four architectures share the same physics and spend their decibels very differently. The diagrams are schematic; the parameter values are the ones used in the model.

MNO macro FWA (T-Mobile, Verizon, AT&T)

A 64T64R massive-MIMO sector on an existing mobile macro site (typically 24 dBi array gain, 320–480 W, 100–160 MHz of C-band or 2.5 GHz) forms a beam to an indoor gateway the customer places at a window. US C-band rules allow 1,640 W/MHz EIRP, so the radio, not the rule, is the limit: about 79 dBm per 100 MHz per beam D. The gateway has 4×4 MIMO but near-zero antenna gain and sits 2 m up behind glass; the uplink is 23–26 dBm from a plastic box.

The commercial engine is capacity, not coverage: T-Mobile forecasts peak mobile load in ~36 million hex bins and sells home internet only where fallow capacity exists, caps the share per bin, and deprioritises home users behind mobile at congestion C. FWA is roughly half of Verizon's traffic for ~3% of service revenue C. Mid-2026: 17–18 M US subscribers across the big three M; address-level availability fell ~2% in big cities in July 2026 as sectors filled M.

Dedicated 5G NR / LTE in CBRS (WISP-grade)

A WISP or rural telco mounts a Category B CBSD (47 dBm/10 MHz EIRP, professional install) on a leased tower and runs 10–150 MHz of General Authorized Access spectrum under a Spectrum Access System. Radios range from 2T2R integrated units (Baicells Aurora 243, 14.5 dBi) to 8T8R and 32T32R macro-class gear; the customer gets a pro-installed 14–17 dBi outdoor panel at the eave, often registered as a Cat A CPE-CBSD (30 dBm/10 MHz) C.

Strength: a multi-vendor 3GPP ecosystem, cheap CPE ($250–400), and spectrum that NTIA counts as "reliable broadband" for BEAD C. Weakness: the sector is one 40–60 MHz carrier in practice, GAA is shared with 430,000+ other CBSDs C, and the fixed panel loses effective gain when the signal arrives via multipath rather than along boresight. Operators report 100 Mbps reliably to ~6 miles with 200–250 ft mounts C.

mmWave 5G FWA (28 / 39 GHz)

Verizon's 2018 launch product: 400–800 MHz channels at 28 GHz from pole-mounted small cells to a window-mounted phased-array receiver. Free-space loss alone is 111 dB at 300 m; standard glass costs 7.6 dB, low-E glass 28.5 dB, a single tree 10–30 dB, and rain 4.6 dB/km at 25 mm/h D. In Sacramento the effective radius was ~700 ft and ~27 eligible addresses per cell C. Verizon pivoted residential FWA to C-band after the 2021 auction and now aims mmWave at multi-dwelling units (Starry acquisition, Oct 2025) C.

In the model the mmWave radio is placed on the same tower top as the others, its best case. It still needs a clutter-free ray; in Georgetown County that exists over water, marsh and the first few hundred metres of town.

Tarana G1 / G2 (ngFWA)

A proprietary, non-3GPP air interface built around distributed massive MIMO: 16 radio chains at the Base Node and 8 at the Remote Node, with beamforming weights recomputed ~5,000 times a second per sub-band so the link uses whatever multipath exists rather than a single boresight ray C. Two cancellation functions are claimed: nulling of the network's own co-channel sectors ("up to 45 dB", allowing every sector to reuse one channel) and asynchronous-burst cancellation of outside interferers such as Wi-Fi ("up to 40 dB") C.

FCC grants pin the hardware down: the G2 BN radiates 48.45 dBm in CBRS (42.4 dBm/10 MHz, below the Cat B cap), 36 dBm in 5/6 GHz; per-port element gain is 13.2–14.3 dBi at 3.5 GHz and 15.6–16.9 dBi at 6 GHz; the RN has 8 cross-polarised 14.5 dBi antennas M. G1: 80 MHz (2×40), 800 Mbps per link, 2.4 Gbps per BN, up to 250 RNs. G2 (Sept 2025): one multiband radio spanning 3.55–3.70, 5.7–5.9 and 5.9–6.9 GHz, four 40 MHz carriers in any band mix, 6.4 Gbps per BN, 512 RNs, backward-compatible with G1 RNs C. Preseem's fleet data across tens of thousands of RNs: median 24-hour link rate 434 Mbps on CBRS, 774 Mbps on 4-carrier 6 GHz M.

Fig. 3

Spectrum each system can use

The uplink is the gate. Every architecture above is asymmetric by rule: a CBRS end-user device may radiate 23 dBm/10 MHz, a CPE-CBSD 30 dBm/10 MHz, a 6 GHz standard-power client 30 dBm total, a C-band handset-class gateway 2 W EIRP, while the base end runs 47–79 dBm. Downlink maps flatter every technology; the 20 Mbps uplink in the 100/20 definition is what actually bounds the footprint, and it is where Tarana's 16-chain receive array matters most.

Read this with the uplink in mind. On downlink alone the MNO macro radio wins in forest: a 79 dBm beam against Tarana's 48.5 dBm carrier is a 30 dB head start, which is why MNO home internet works with an indoor box at all. But the same trees sit on the return path from a 29 dBm gateway, and the 20 Mbps uplink in the 100/20 definition fails long before the downlink does. Benchmarks behind the sliders

Busy-hour demand: OpenVault Q2 2026 average 711 GB/month ≈ 2.2 Mbps continuous; with ~10% of daily traffic in the busy hour that is ≈5.2 Mbps per subscriber D. Tab 8

Where Tarana sits among proprietary fixed wireless

Tarana is not the only non-3GPP radio; it is the only one built from scratch for non-line-of-sight. The rest of the category is 802.11-derived or TDMA point-to-multipoint that wants a clear path, and in September 2026 the category's largest vendor failed.

Cambium Networks collapsed on 11 September 2026 (more than half its staff cut, UK entity in administration, Nasdaq delisting in August). On 22 September Airspan bought PMP 450, PTP 670/700, cnWave 60 GHz, cnReach and the LINKPlanner/cnHeat/cnMaestro tools with 135 staff; ePMP, the volume product, was excluded and has no buyer. Per Preseem, Cambium gear sat behind 39.5% of US fixed-wireless CPE and 28.5% of access points; Tarana was 8.3% of the base but "taking the growth spend" C. Airspan itself had sold Mimosa to Radisys in 2023. Any FWA diligence now carries an orphaned-vendor scenario.

Prices are US/CA reseller list in Oct 2026 and move with promotions; ranges are planning values from vendor material and operator reports. C

Head-to-head evidence

Tarana's own Michigan CBRS test against an 8×8 MU-MIMO competitor (consistent with a PMP 450m) reported 266 vs 99 Mbps at 1.85 miles LOS, 210 vs 19 Mbps under interference at 2.47 miles, and 257 Mbps versus no link at 2.16 miles NLOS and 5.96 miles; the G1 used 80 MHz against the competitor's 40 MHz C. No independent, instrumented Tarana-versus-5G-NR comparison exists in the trade press. Operator statements are consistent in direction: Resound reports 40% fewer towers and 6× subscribers per tower; Wisper dropped 300 planned towers for ~$5M/year of opex C.

What the category tells you about Tarana risk

Proprietary FWA has always been a two-sided bet: the radio and the company. Cambium's installed base will keep running (the radios work without the vendor until the cloud controller or spares run out), which is the realistic failure mode to model for Tarana as well: Tarana Cloud Suite is the hosted NMS for activation, firmware and SLA profiles. Tarana has raised about $457M; its share price has been flat at $1.07 since 2023 across three rounds and there is no IPO filing C. Revenue was "nearly $100M" in the year to September 2023 C.

Tab 9

Starlink as the yardstick

Every rural fixed-wireless sale in 2026 is made against a dish the customer can order tonight. Starlink is now a top-ten US ISP, won 48% of South Carolina's BEAD locations with Kuiper, and sets the price and speed expectations a WISP has to beat.

Where Starlink is weaker

Capacity is sold per cell, exactly like an MNO sector: waitlists were replaced in 2026 by one-time "demand surcharges" of $100–1,500 by location M. Median upload sits at 20 Mbps or less in most states; only 44.7% of users met 100/20 in Q4 2025, up from 17.4% a year earlier M. Latency is 25–60 ms and physics-bound. Trees matter here too: a dish needs an unobstructed view of a large sky arc, and in pine country that often means a mast or a roof peak, which erodes the self-install advantage. The V3 satellite and Starship capacity step-change remains unconfirmed in orbit as of this writing.

Where Starlink is stronger

Zero towers, zero backhaul, zero spectrum fights, national marketing, and a cost-per-location in BEAD ($1,383 national average; $1,920 in South Carolina) that no terrestrial build matches at low density M. Terrestrial FWA wins where density supports a sector (roughly 100+ passings per sector at ordinary take rates), where the upload matters (video calls, cameras, backups), and where an operator wants a network it controls. The companion Starlink Mobile analysis covers the direct-to-cell side of this story.

Propagation pipeline

Terrain: USGS 3DEP 1 arc-second (≈30 m) elevation, resampled to a 90 m UTM 17N grid. Land cover: NLCD 2021. Towers: FCC Antenna Structure Registration weekly extract (4 Oct 2026), county FIPS 45043, status Constructed; three selected for clutter diversity. Housing: 2020 Census block housing counts (TIGER 2023 TABBLOCK20), distributed evenly over each block's cells.

Path loss: ITS Irregular Terrain Model v1.2.2 (Longley-Rice) in point-to-point mode via the itmlogic port, median (50% time, 50% location, 50% confidence), vertical polarisation, continental temperate climate, Ns = 314, average ground (ε = 15, σ = 0.005 S/m), 360 radials per tower, 90 m steps to 25 km, at 2.6, 3.6 and 6.0 GHz and CPE heights of 2, 6 and 10 m; free-space inside 1 km. Other bands are shifted by 20 log(f/flayer). 28 GHz is outside ITM's range and uses free-space loss plus gaseous and rain terms with a clutter line-of-sight test.

Clutter: each NLCD class carries a representative obstacle height (pine 22 m, mixed 21 m, deciduous 20 m, swamp forest 18 m, developed 5–15 m, shrub 3 m). Along every radial the ray from the antenna to the CPE is tested against terrain plus obstacle height with 4/3-earth bulge; foliage depth is the length of forested cells in the obstructed run adjacent to the CPE, plus half a cell when the CPE itself stands inside canopy. Foliage loss uses ITU-R P.833 (A = Am(1−e−dγ/Am), Am = 1.37 fMHz0.42, γ scaled from P.833 Table 1 as 0.34(f/2117)0.8 dB/m). Developed classes add an ITU-R P.2108 low-angle median term (23.5 + 9.6 log fGHz) scaled by intensity (0.5/0.8/1.0/1.2) and by the CPE's height below the roof line. Indoor gateways add 3GPP TR 38.901 §7.4.3 penetration. Leaf-off multiplies deciduous (0.7), mixed (0.8), swamp forest (0.85) and shrub (0.9).

Link budget: SINR = EIRP + Grx − L − penalty − (−174 + 10 log B + NF) − fade − interference margin. Spectral efficiency 0.8·log2(1+SINR) capped at the technology's peak, zero below −5 dB; rank 1/2/4 layers at <12 / <20 / ≥20 dB capped by the CPE; uplink picks the sub-band split (1 to 1/16 of the carrier) that maximises rate. Fixed-beam CPEs lose a user-set 4 dB of effective gain in NLOS (angular spread); indoor gateways 1 dB; adaptive arrays 0 dB.

Limits

What the model does not do

It is a median model. Location variability (σ ≈ 8 dB in forest per P.833) is folded into a single fade margin instead of being carried as a distribution, so edges are sharper than reality. It has no building footprints; clutter at the CPE is inferred from the 30 m land-cover class, which under-represents the clearing around a rural house and over-represents trees on a developed parcel. Foliage constants are extrapolated from 0.1–2.1 GHz measurements with one 3.6 GHz anchor and none at 6 GHz; a Lowcountry drive test is the single most valuable calibration (item 14 on the previous tab). Multipath exploitation by adaptive arrays is represented only as the absence of the fixed-panel NLOS penalty. Capacity, scheduling and interference between the three sites are handled on the economics tab, not in the map.

The mmWave case is deliberately generous (tower-top mounting) and still fails; a pole-mounted small-cell grid would need its own study. The MNO case assumes the macro site carries a C-band or n41 FWA sector pointed at the county, which is true for the Georgetown site and plausible for the other two; it does not model the fallow-capacity gate that decides whether an address is actually offered service. Starlink is not mapped because its coverage is not a propagation question. Finally, Georgetown County itself is close to fully served by fiber and cable after the HTC and Spectrum builds (rural unserved households fell from 27% to under 3%); the county is used as a physics test bed, and the results transfer to any pine-belt county, not as a business case for this county.
